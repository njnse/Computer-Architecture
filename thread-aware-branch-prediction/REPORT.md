# Shared Branch Predictor Interference

## Research question

At equal counter capacity, when does partitioning predictor counters by hardware thread reduce destructive aliasing, and when does the resulting capacity reduction hurt? Can isolating history alone account for the benefit?

This artifact implements a complete controlled experiment, not a proposed experiment. It is a research baseline for a potential master's thesis on dynamically allocating predictor resources.

## Model and policies

The predictor is a gshare-style XOR index into two-bit saturating counters, initialized weakly not taken. Each branch is predicted and updated immediately in committed trace order. There are four thread identifiers. The PC shifts right by two before indexing; branch addresses are aligned to four bytes.

Three policies separate history contamination from table contamination:

1. Shared-global: one counter table and one global history register.
2. Shared-local: one counter table and a history register per thread.
3. Partitioned-local: four equal counter-table partitions and a history register per thread.

Every organization has 32, 128, or 512 total counters. History lengths are 0, 4, or 8 bits. Consequently counter storage is equal but total storage is not precisely equal: private history uses four times the global-history bits. Both costs are recorded in each CSV row. In partitioned-local, the effective index masks to the partition width; history bits above that width do not affect indexing.

This is not a speculative SMT frontend. It does not model branch resolution delay, wrong-path updates, rollback, instruction fetch, context-switch flushes, BTB entries, indirect branches, predictor latency, energy, or program runtime.

## Workloads and provenance

All traces are generated by the checked-in source using Python's standard pseudo-random generator. No third-party traces or restricted benchmark data are used.

- Opposing-bias: the same PC in all threads, with 95% taken probability in even-numbered threads and 5% in odd-numbered threads. This intentionally exposes cross-thread aliasing.
- Shared-bias: all threads use one PC with 95% taken probability. This is a constructive-sharing control.
- Capacity-pressure: thread zero accesses 256 aligned branch sites uniformly, with per-site biases chosen from 5% and 95%. Each other thread accesses one distinct site with 95% taken probability. This deliberately uneven occupancy tests whether pooled counters help a thread with a large working set.
- Thread-alternation: each thread's single branch alternates deterministically. This separates history effects from simple frequency learning.

Threads switch after 1, 16, or 256 branches. The quantum is a controlled interleaving parameter, not an operating-system time slice. Every trace contains 10,240 branches: 2,048 warmup branches followed by 8,192 measured branches. Each thread contributes exactly 2,048 measured branches. Five trace seeds are shared across policies. For a given regime, quantum, and seed, all organizations see the identical trace.

Four regimes × three quanta × three capacities × three history lengths × three organizations × five seeds produce 1,620 records. This is exploratory work: after an initial screening, the capacity workload was revised from equal occupancy to uneven occupancy to isolate capacity fragmentation. All final configurations were then rerun. The illustrative summary slice stayed unchanged, but this is not a preregistered study.

## Measurements and interpretation

The generated [measurement table](results/MEASUREMENTS.md) reports the predefined slice of 128 counters, four history bits, and quantum one. The [raw CSV](results/raw.csv) contains every configuration and per-thread count; the [JSON manifest](results/summary.json) records means and seed ranges. Error rates count mispredicted measured branches divided by measured branches.

| Workload | Shared-global | Shared-local | Partitioned-local |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Opposing bias | 6.250% | 6.252% | 5.552% |
| Shared bias | 5.349% | 5.356% | 5.444% |
| Uneven capacity pressure | 14.351% | 16.306% | 16.567% |
| Thread alternation | 0.000% | 0.000% | 0.000% |

Partitioning reduces the opposing-bias error by 0.698 percentage points relative to shared-global in this slice. Merely isolating history does not produce that reduction. The result is modest, and the noiseless alternation control has no remaining measured errors after warmup.

A diagnostic slice isolates capacity fragmentation: with 512 counters, zero history bits, and quantum one, shared-global and shared-local both have mean error 5.767%, while partitioned-local has 11.274%. At zero history, the two shared policies are identical and the heavy thread can use the pooled table; hard partitioning limits it to 128 entries. This supports the counter-capacity tradeoff without attributing it to history contamination. This diagnostic slice was inspected after the sweep and is exploratory.

The trace model can establish whether resource sharing changes prediction error in its synthetic workloads. It cannot translate percentage-point error reduction into IPC or claim benefits for commercial SMT processors. No synthetic runtime conversion is applied.

The opposing-bias case is deliberately favorable to partitioning, while shared-bias is a control. Capacity-pressure demonstrates why complete isolation must be compared with resource pooling. Thread-alternation tests whether private history rather than table partitioning explains an improvement.

Worst-thread error percentages are included to expose asymmetric harm. Five seeds quantify variability in the generator; they do not constitute evidence about the distribution of real software.

## Validation

Seven tests check saturation and initial prediction, partition isolation, thread-history isolation, history-width masking, a deterministic opposing-stream counterexample, equivalence of shared global/local policies with zero history, and trace reproducibility with balanced measured counts.

The exact counterexample uses a zero-history alternating sequence of opposite outcomes at one PC. It produces 100 errors with a shared weakly-not-taken counter and one error with separated counters. This validates a specific mechanism rather than merely checking that the code runs.

## Limitations and related work

Equal counter budgets do not imply equal total area. Implementable designs must account for history storage, thread identification, allocation logic, access ports, and timing. Hard partitioning also presumes a fixed number of active threads; the present model does not redistribute inactive-thread capacity.

Modern predictors such as TAGE-family designs are stronger baselines than this small gshare-style model. The experiment establishes a controlled mechanism and tradeoff, not superiority over state-of-the-art predictors.

Live academic literature verification was unavailable for this artifact. The novelty assessment is provisional, and no checked bibliography or claim of priority is supplied. A thesis review must cover SMT branch predictor interference, per-thread histories, tagged predictors, dynamic resource partitioning, and modern predictor allocation. Gshare and thread partitioning are established techniques; novelty cannot rest on reproducing them.

## Thesis extension and venue

A candidate contribution is a bounded online interference estimator that redistributes a shared predictor budget among active threads, while keeping hot low-interference entries shared. The hard-partition and history-only policies here provide ablations.

The next implementation should measure destructive updates directly, account for tags and allocation costs, and test unseen workload mixes with phase changes. It should compare against modern tagged predictors, include an equal-total-bit-budget experiment, and validate selected outcomes in a speculative multicore frontend or a suitable full CPU simulator.

MICRO or HPCA could be appropriate if an implementable allocation mechanism yields robust prediction and execution benefits under strict storage/timing constraints. This artifact is a runnable starting point, not a conference-ready paper or a completed master's thesis.
